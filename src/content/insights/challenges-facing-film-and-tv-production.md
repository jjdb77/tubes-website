Meet Sara. She runs a small production company somewhere in Europe with four people on the payroll and two projects that matter this year. One is a feature film, a drama with a director she has worked with before. The other is a documentary series that a broadcaster liked enough to ask for a second meeting. Sara is fictional. Her year is not. Ask any independent producer and they will recognise most of it.

## January. The commission that takes longer

The documentary series looked close. The commissioning editor liked the pitch, the taster tape went down well, and then the answer became "not yet". The channel wants to see how the current slate performs first. The streamer Sara also approached is commissioning less than it did a few years ago and now asks for projects that are further along, ideally with talent attached and part of the money already in place.

That is the first change of this period. After years in which streamers raced each other for content, the spending race has cooled. Buyers are more careful, decisions take longer and fewer projects get through. For a producer it means more development work done at her own risk, and more months between the first pitch and the first invoice. The [development](/how-a-film-is-made/#development) stage, which never paid well, now has to be carried for longer.

## March. Less of the series is hers

When the broadcaster finally says yes, the offer covers most of the budget, but in return it wants more rights for longer, and in more territories. Sara wanted to keep the international sales and a possible format version, because those rights are what turn a production company into a business with value of its own. Without them, every production is a service job. She makes a fee, the buyer keeps the upside.

This is the second pressure. Fully funded commissions are attractive because the money is certain, but they often come with a buyout of the rights. Keeping rights usually means putting money in yourself, through [deficit financing](/how-a-film-is-made/#tv-commission) from distributors, funds and incentives. That makes the project more valuable and much harder to finance.

## May. A finance plan with eleven pieces

The feature has a budget of a few million euros, and no single financier will cover more than a slice. Sara's finance plan has a national film fund, a regional fund, a co-producer in another country with access to that country's fund, a pre-sale to a broadcaster, a minimum guarantee from a sales agent, a cash rebate and a bit of private equity. Each piece comes with its own conditions, its own deadline and its own reporting. The regional fund wants a share of the budget spent in its region. The co-production treaty sets how much each country must contribute. The sales agent wants a cast name that the fund's jury does not care about.

[Financing](/how-a-film-is-made/#financing) an independent film has always looked like this, but there are more pieces than before and they take longer to close. Every month of delay is a month in which the cast's availability, the crew and the season can slip away. The [overview of film funds and investors](/compare-film-investors/) on this site exists because finding all those pieces is a job in itself.

## July. The budget no longer fits the money

By the time the financing is in place, the budget Sara wrote a year earlier is out of date. Crew rates went up, insurance went up, so did travel, accommodation, energy and equipment. The financiers, however, committed to the old number. The gap has to come out of the film itself. Two shooting days are cut, a location is replaced by a studio set, the contingency is smaller than she would like.

In many places production costs have risen while the money available to make films has not kept pace, and buyers rarely raise their price to match. The result is tighter budgets with less room for the unexpected. That is exactly when a [realistic contingency](/insights/how-much-contingency-does-a-production-need/) and a current [cost report](/insights/what-is-a-production-cost-report/) stop being paperwork and become the way a producer keeps control.

## August. Finding the crew

The shoot is planned for the autumn, and Sara cannot get her first choice of director of photography, gaffer or production designer. In busy periods the experienced people are booked by the large productions. In quiet periods many of them are idle, and some leave the industry for steadier work. Both happen in the same year.

For a small production this means compromises in experience, higher rates for the people who are available, or moving the shoot. The [roles guide](/production-roles/) shows how many specialists a film needs. Losing one key person can ripple through a whole department.

## October. Waiting for the rebate

The shoot goes well. The cash rebate covers a solid share of the local spend, but it is paid after the production is finished, the costs are audited and the authority has checked everything. That can take many months. In the meantime the crew, the suppliers and the post house need to be paid.

Sara bridges the gap with a loan against the rebate, which costs interest and fees, or with a deferment from suppliers who are willing to wait. An incentive that is worth thirty percent on paper is worth less once the cost of waiting for it is counted. The [incentive comparison](/compare-film-incentives/) shows when each scheme pays out for that reason.

## November. The AI question

In post, the questions are new. Can the editor use AI tools to clean up dialogue? Is a generated background in a VFX shot fine for the broadcaster? What do the actors' contracts say about their voice and likeness? The sales agent asks for a warranty that no AI was used in a way that could cause rights problems, and the insurer wants to know too.

AI is already saving time in subtitles, transcription, previs and some post work. At the same time the rules on consent, copyright and what buyers will accept are still being written, and they differ per country and per buyer. For a producer the risk is less the technology than the paperwork. A production now has to be able to show what was used where, and with whose permission.

## December. More reporting, less time

At the end of the year Sara counts the reports she sent. Cost reports for the financiers. A spend report for the regional fund. A sustainability plan and a carbon calculation, which more and more funds and broadcasters now ask for. A final cost statement, audited, for the rebate. Delivery paperwork for the sales agent. Each wants the same numbers in a different shape.

Every one of those reports is reasonable on its own. Together they take days that a small company does not have, and a mistake in one can delay a payment in another.

## What the producers who come through it have in common

None of these challenges goes away by working harder. What the producers who get through this period have in common is that they know where their money stands at any moment. They know what is spent, what is committed and what is still to come. They can show a financier a current estimate for completion without a week of spreadsheets. When something has to give, whether a shooting day, a location or a piece of the finance plan, they can see the consequences before they decide.

That is not glamorous, but it is what lets them say yes to the next project with confidence. New to these terms? Read [how a film is made](/how-a-film-is-made/), from first idea to release.
