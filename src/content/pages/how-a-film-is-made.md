    heading: "Not every production is a feature film"
    lead: This guide follows an independent feature, but the road depends on what you make and who pays for it.
    text: |
      ### Studio films and indie films

      A **studio film** is made and paid for by a big studio or streamer that owns the result. The studio decides, finances it from its own money and handles the release itself.

      An **independent film**, or **indie film**, is made outside that system. A production company raises the money piece by piece from investors, film funds, broadcasters, distributors and tax incentives, and keeps more creative control in return. Budgets are usually smaller and the [financing](#financing) takes longer. Most European films are independent films, and this guide follows that route. "Indie" says nothing about the subject or the style. It is about who owns and finances the film.

      ### Scripted

      **Scripted** means everything that is written in advance and performed by actors, such as feature films, drama series, comedy and soaps. The story is fixed in the script before the shoot, so the [screenwriter](/production-roles/screenwriter/) and the [script editor](/production-roles/script-editor/) matter early, and most of the money goes to cast, crew and shooting days. The stages on this page apply almost one to one.

      ### Unscripted

      **Unscripted** covers productions with real people instead of actors, such as documentaries, factual series, reality, docusoaps, game shows and entertainment. There is no script. Development is a **format**, a short description of how the show works, sometimes with a **taster tape** to show the tone. Instead of actors, the team casts **contributors**, the real people on screen.

      The biggest difference is that the story is found in the edit. A shoot can produce hundreds of hours of material, and [story producers](/production-roles/story-producer/) and editors shape it into episodes afterwards. That makes post-production a much bigger share of the budget than on a drama. A [series producer](/production-roles/series-producer/) runs the whole run, and [researchers](/production-roles/researcher/) find stories and contributors. A successful format can be sold abroad and remade in other countries.

      A **documentary** sits in between. It follows real events, often over years, with a small crew, and is often financed like an indie film with film funds and broadcasters.

      ### Live, animation and commercials

      **Live** television, such as news, sport and live shows, is mostly made in-house by the broadcaster in a studio or with an outside broadcast unit, led by a [studio director](/production-roles/studio-director/). **Animation** has no shoot. The production stage is the long work of designing, animating and rendering, and can take years. **Commercials** and branded content are paid for by a brand and follow their own route, described in [how a commercial is made](#commercials).

    theme: light

    heading: Who is involved
    lead: A film has many owners of a small piece. Knowing who they are explains most of what happens later.
    plain_list: true
    two_column: true
    text: |
      To keep it concrete, picture a fictional feature film, a coastal drama we will call *Low Tide*, with a budget of a few million euros. These are the parties around it.

      - **The producer and the production company.** The [producer](/production-roles/producer/) starts the project, owns the rights, raises the money and answers for the result. The production company is the business that signs the contracts and employs the crew.
      - **The writer and the director.** The [screenwriter](/production-roles/screenwriter/) writes the script. The [director](/production-roles/director/) decides how it looks and sounds on screen.
      - **Equity investors.** Private people or companies who put in money in exchange for a share of the profits.
      - **Film funds.** Public bodies, national or regional, that support films with grants or loans because they want culture or jobs in their area.
      - **Broadcasters and streamers.** TV channels and streaming services that pay up front for the right to show the film later.
      - **Distributors and sales agents.** A [distributor](/production-roles/distributor/) brings the film to audiences in one country. A [sales agent](/production-roles/sales-agent/) sells it to distributors in many countries. Both often commit money before the film exists, through pre-sales and minimum guarantees (explained under Financing).
      - **Banks.** Lend money against signed contracts, so the producer can spend money that will only arrive later.
      - **Incentive authorities.** Government offices that give back part of what a production spends in their country, as a tax credit or cash rebate.
      - **Co-producers.** A [co-producer](/production-roles/co-producer/) is a partner company, often in another country, that brings in money, crew or access to local funds.
      - **The completion guarantor.** A [completion guarantor](/production-roles/completion-guarantor/) is an insurer of sorts who promises the financiers that the film will be finished and delivered.
      - **Talent agents.** They represent actors, writers and directors and negotiate their fees and conditions.
      - **The audience.** In the end everyone above is betting on whether people will watch.
  - type: textblock
    theme: white
    anchor: development
    heading: Development
    lead: The stage where an idea becomes a project someone wants to pay for. It often takes several years.
    text: |
      It starts with an idea. That can be an original story, but often it is a book, an article or a true event. If the story belongs to someone else, the producer first needs the **rights**. Usually the producer buys an **option**, a fee that reserves the rights for a set period. If the film gets made, the full price is paid. If not, the option runs out and the owner can sell it again.

      Next comes a **treatment**, a short prose version of the story, and then the screenplay. A script goes through many **drafts**, with notes from the producer and often a [script editor](/production-roles/script-editor/). For *Low Tide* that could mean four or five versions before anyone outside the company reads it.

      All this costs money before there is any budget. **Development money** comes from the production company itself, from film funds with development schemes, or from a broadcaster or distributor who wants first look at the project.

      The last step is **packaging**. The producer attaches a director and one or two well-known actors, because financiers invest in a package, not just a script. A good script with the right names attached is what opens the doors to the next stage.

      Development is led by the [producer](/production-roles/producer/), often together with a [development producer](/production-roles/development-producer/) or a [head of development](/production-roles/head-of-development/) at a larger company.
  - type: textblock
    theme: light
    anchor: financing
    heading: Financing
    lead: Very few films are paid for by one party. The producer builds a jigsaw of sources, each with its own conditions.
    text: |
      The producer writes a **finance plan**, a list of every source of money and how much each brings in. Together they must cover the full budget. For *Low Tide* the plan might look like this.

      - **Equity.** Money from investors who get a share of the profits in return.
      - **Pre-sales.** A distributor in one country pays for the right to release the film there, before it is made.
      - **Minimum guarantee (MG).** An advance from a distributor or sales agent. They pay a fixed sum now and keep the film's income until they have earned it back.
      - **Gap financing.** A loan against territories that have not been sold yet, at a higher risk and a higher price.
      - **Soft money.** Grants and loans from film funds that do not need to be paid back in full, or only from profits.
      - **Tax incentives and cash rebates.** Many countries give back a percentage of what is spent locally. You can compare them in our [incentive comparison](/compare-film-incentives/) and read each scheme in the [incentive guide](/film-incentives/).

      A film shot in two countries can become an official **co-production** under a treaty between those countries, so it qualifies for funds and incentives in both. Money that arrives late, such as a pre-sale paid on delivery, can be borrowed against from a **bank**, with the signed contract as security.

      Financiers then usually ask for a **completion bond**, a guarantee from a [completion guarantor](/production-roles/completion-guarantor/) that the film will be finished on budget, or that they get their money back.

      Every financier agrees in what order they are repaid, the **recoupment order**. In plain words, it is the queue for the money once the film earns something.

      For TV the route is often simpler. A broadcaster or streamer can **commission** the project and pay most of the cost, see [how a TV commission works](#tv-commission).

      Financing is led by the [producer](/production-roles/producer/) and the [executive producer](/production-roles/executive-producer/), with a [production lawyer](/production-roles/production-lawyer/) drafting the contracts.
  - type: textblock
    theme: white
    anchor: pre-production
    heading: Pre-production
    lead: The money is in place. Now every day of the shoot gets planned, because changing things later costs far more.
    text: |
      The [line producer](/production-roles/line-producer/) turns the script into a detailed **budget** and a **shooting schedule**. Once the financiers approve them, the budget is **locked**, meaning it becomes the fixed plan the production is measured against. If you want to see what such a budget contains, look at our [film budget template](/film-budget-template/) or build one in the [Budget Builder](/tools/budget-builder/).

      Then the team grows fast.

      - **Hiring.** Heads of department such as the [director of photography](/production-roles/director-of-photography/) and the [production designer](/production-roles/production-designer/) are hired, and they bring their own teams.
      - **Casting.** The [casting director](/production-roles/casting-director/) fills the remaining roles.
      - **Locations.** The [location manager](/production-roles/location-manager/) finds and books places to shoot. A **recce** is the visit where director and heads of department see a location together and decide how to use it.
      - **Prep.** Sets are built, costumes are made, equipment is tested.
      - **Insurance.** The production insures the cast, the equipment and the shoot itself.

      The [first assistant director](/production-roles/first-assistant-director/) breaks the script down scene by scene and turns it into a day-by-day plan. The [production manager](/production-roles/production-manager/) runs the production office, where contracts, bookings and purchase orders come together.

      Pre-production is led by the [line producer](/production-roles/line-producer/), working closely with the director and the producer.
  - type: textblock
    theme: light
    anchor: production
    heading: Production
    lead: The shoot, also called principal photography. The crew is at its largest and every day costs a lot of money.
    text: |
      A film is shot in **shoot days**, not in story order. *Low Tide* might shoot all its harbour scenes in one week and all its interiors in another, because moving a whole crew is expensive.

      Every evening the [second assistant director](/production-roles/second-assistant-director/) sends out the **call sheet**, the plan for the next day. It says who needs to be where and when, which scenes are shot and what is needed for them.

      The footage of each day is checked by the director and producers as **dailies** (in the US also called rushes). They want to know early if a scene needs to be shot again.

      The production office writes a **daily progress report**, a record of what was shot, how long it took and anything that went wrong. Each week the [production accountant](/production-roles/production-accountant/) puts the actual costs next to the budget in a **cost report**, which shows where the money is going and what the film is likely to cost in the end. Our article on the [production cost report](/insights/what-is-a-production-cost-report/) explains how it works.

      Things go over. A day runs long and the crew is paid **overtime**, or a location falls through. For that the budget holds a **contingency**, a reserve of a few percent that is only released with approval. Read more in [how much contingency a production needs](/insights/how-much-contingency-does-a-production-need/).

      On set, the [director](/production-roles/director/) leads the creative work and the [first assistant director](/production-roles/first-assistant-director/) runs the day. The [line producer](/production-roles/line-producer/) keeps the shoot on schedule and on budget.
  - type: textblock
    theme: white
    anchor: post-production
    heading: Post-production
    lead: The footage becomes a film. This stage often takes longer than the shoot.
    text: |
      The [editor](/production-roles/editor/) has usually started during the shoot, assembling scenes as the footage comes in. After the shoot, editor and director shape the film over many weeks. When nobody will change the cut any more, the film reaches **picture lock**. From then on the length and order of every shot are fixed, so the other specialists can do their work.

      - **VFX.** Visual effects, from removing a modern sign in the background to creating a storm at sea, supervised by a [VFX supervisor](/production-roles/vfx-supervisor/).
      - **Grade.** The [colourist](/production-roles/colourist/) sets the colour and mood of every shot so the film looks like one whole.
      - **Sound.** The [sound designer](/production-roles/sound-designer/) builds the sound world, dialogue is cleaned up and the [re-recording mixer](/production-roles/re-recording-mixer/) balances everything in the final mix.
      - **Music.** A [composer](/production-roles/composer/) writes the score, and a [music supervisor](/production-roles/music-supervisor/) finds and licenses existing songs.
      - **Titles.** Opening titles and end credits are designed and checked against every contract, because many people have a right to a credit in a certain place.

      Post-production is led by the [post-production supervisor](/production-roles/post-production-supervisor/), who keeps schedule and costs on track, with the director and producer approving each step.
  - type: textblock
    theme: light
    anchor: delivery
    heading: Delivery
    lead: A finished film is not yet a sellable film. It has to be delivered, and that is a project of its own.
    text: |
      Every distributor and sales agent has a **delivery schedule**, also called delivery requirements. It is a long list of what they need to receive before they pay what they owe. Some of it is technical.

      - **Masters.** The final version of the film in the formats they ask for, for cinema, television and streaming.
      - **Deliverables.** Separate sound tracks so the film can be dubbed, subtitles, trailers, stills from the [unit stills photographer](/production-roles/unit-stills-photographer/) and marketing material.

      Much of it is legal. The **chain of title** is the set of documents that proves the producer owns all the rights, from the option on the book to every music licence. **E&O insurance** (errors and omissions) covers claims that the film used something without permission, such as a name, a logo or a song.

      Film funds and incentive authorities usually also want a **cost statement**, an overview of what was really spent, often checked by an independent auditor. Only after that do they pay out their final instalment.

      For *Low Tide*, a part of the financing only arrives after delivery is accepted. That is why producers treat delivery as seriously as the shoot.

      Delivery is led by the [producer](/production-roles/producer/) and the [post-production supervisor](/production-roles/post-production-supervisor/), with the [production accountant](/production-roles/production-accountant/) preparing the cost statement.
  - type: textblock
    theme: white
    anchor: release
    heading: Release
    lead: The film meets its audience, one country and one platform at a time.
    text: |
      Many independent films start at a **festival**. A good festival slot gives a film attention, reviews and buyers. You can browse the big ones in our [festival comparison](/compare-film-festivals/).

      Alongside festivals run **sales markets**, trade fairs where sales agents meet distributors from all over the world and sell the rights country by country. A [sales agent](/production-roles/sales-agent/) may sell *Low Tide* to a distributor in France, another in Germany and a streaming service for a group of smaller countries.

      Each [distributor](/production-roles/distributor/) then releases the film in its territory. They pay for **marketing** and **P&A** (prints and advertising), the cost of copies, trailers, posters and advertising. They also decide on the release date and the number of cinemas.

      A film usually moves through **release windows**, periods in which it is only available in one place.

      - **Cinema.** The first window, for films that get a theatrical release.
      - **VOD.** Video on demand, where you rent or buy a single film online.
      - **Streaming.** A subscription service that offers the film as part of its catalogue.
      - **TV.** Pay TV first, free television later.

      The order and length of these windows differ per country and per deal, and they have become shorter over the years.

      The release is led by the distributors in each country. The producer and the [unit publicist](/production-roles/unit-publicist/) support it with materials and the cast's press work.
  - type: textblock
    theme: light
    anchor: after-release
    heading: After release
    lead: Once the film earns money, it flows back through a queue. Who stands where in that queue was agreed years earlier.
    text: |
      Income from cinemas, platforms and TV deals does not go straight to the producer. Distributors first keep their fee and their costs. What is left travels up to the sales agent, who also keeps a fee and earns back any minimum guarantee.

      Larger productions use a **collection account**, an independent company that receives all income and pays it out according to the agreements. That way no single party holds the money.

      The order of payment is called the **recoupment waterfall**. Picture water filling a row of buckets, one after another. The first bucket might be the bank loan and the gap financing. Then the equity investors get their money back, sometimes with a premium. Only when all those buckets are full does the rest count as **profit**, which is split between investors, producer and anyone else with a share.

      Some people get paid regardless of profit. **Residuals** are extra payments to cast and crew when the film is shown again, for example on TV, under union or collective agreements in some countries. **Royalties** go to rights holders such as the author of the book or the composer of the music.

      **Profit participation** is a share of the profits promised in a contract, often to the director or a lead actor. Because so much is paid out first, many films never reach that point.

      This stage is followed up by the [producer](/production-roles/producer/), with the [production accountant](/production-roles/production-accountant/) or a [business affairs manager](/production-roles/business-affairs-manager/) checking the statements that come in.
  - type: textblock
    theme: white
    anchor: tv-commission
    heading: How a TV commission works
    lead: Most television is not financed like a film. A broadcaster or streamer orders it and pays most of the bill.
    text: |
      An independent production company has an idea for a series. It pitches it to a [commissioning editor](/production-roles/commissioning-editor/), the person at a broadcaster or streamer who decides which programmes get made for a certain genre or slot.

      If the commissioning editor is interested, there is often a **development deal** first. The broadcaster pays for scripts or a pilot episode, and keeps the right to decide whether to continue.

      If the answer is yes, the broadcaster gives a **commission**. It pays a **licence fee**, the price for the right to show the series for a period and in certain territories. That fee does not always cover the whole budget. The gap is called the **deficit**, and the production company fills it with **deficit financing**, for example from a distributor, a co-producer or a tax incentive.

      In return the production company often keeps some rights, such as selling the series abroad or making a format version for other countries. Those rights are what turns a production company into a business with value of its own. How much it keeps depends on the deal and on the country.

      During the production a [production executive](/production-roles/production-executive/) from the broadcaster follows budget and progress. At the end the series is **delivered** to the broadcaster's technical specifications and passes **compliance**, a check by a [compliance manager](/production-roles/compliance-manager/) that it meets broadcasting rules and the channel's own standards.

      On the production side, a TV project is led by the executive producer and, on drama, a [showrunner](/production-roles/showrunner/) or [series producer](/production-roles/series-producer/).
  - type: textblock
    theme: light
    anchor: commercials
    heading: How a commercial is made
    lead: A commercial runs on the same crafts as a film, but the money comes from a brand and the process is built around approvals.
    text: |
      It starts with a **brief**. A [brand manager](/production-roles/brand-manager/) at a company tells its advertising agency what the campaign should achieve. At the agency, a [creative director](/production-roles/creative-director/) and a team including a [copywriter](/production-roles/copywriter/) and an [art director](/production-roles/art-director/) come up with the **creative idea**, usually a script or a storyboard.

      Once the client approves the idea, the [agency producer](/production-roles/agency-producer/) approaches a few production companies and their directors. Each [director](/production-roles/director/) writes a **treatment**, a visual document of how they would make it.

      The production companies then send a **bid**, a quote for the job. In commercials this is usually a **fixed price**, so if the shoot costs more than the bid, that is the production company's problem. The agency and the client choose one, and the job is **awarded**.

      In pre-production everything is agreed with the client at the **PPM** (pre-production meeting), from cast and locations to the look of each shot. Then comes the shoot, often in one or a few days.

      In post, the **offline edit** is the rough version for approval and the **online edit** is the finished, graded version. The client approves at several points along the way. The final versions are delivered to the **media**, the TV channels, cinemas and online platforms where the ad will run.

      Last come **usage rights**. Actors and music are paid for a set use, such as a period, a set of countries and a set of media. A **buyout** is a payment that covers that use in one go.

      At the production company, a commercial is led by the [commercials producer](/production-roles/commercials-producer/), with the [head of production](/production-roles/head-of-production/) watching costs across all jobs.

    items:
      - question: How long does it take to make a film?
        answer: |
          It varies a lot. Development is often the longest part and can take several years. Financing can overlap with that. Pre-production, the shoot and post-production together often take around a year for an independent feature, and release follows after that. A commercial can go from brief to broadcast in a matter of weeks.
      - question: How are films financed?
        answer: |
          Usually from several sources at once. A typical independent film combines equity from investors, pre-sales to distributors, a minimum guarantee from a sales agent, support from film funds and a tax incentive or cash rebate. A bank can lend against contracts that only pay out later. See the [financing](#financing) section for each source.
      - question: What is the difference between pre-production and production?
        answer: |
          Pre-production is the planning. The budget is locked, the crew is hired, locations are booked and every shooting day is scheduled. Production is the shoot itself, when the cameras roll. The better the planning, the fewer expensive surprises during the shoot.
      - question: What is a completion bond?
        answer: |
          A guarantee for the financiers that the film will be finished and delivered. It is issued by a completion guarantor, who watches the production closely. If the film runs into serious trouble, the guarantor can step in with extra money or, in the worst case, take over the production.
      - question: What is recoupment?
        answer: |
          Earning back the money that was put into the film. Each financier agrees beforehand in what order they are repaid from the film's income. That order is called the recoupment order or waterfall, and only after everyone in it has been repaid does the film make a profit.
      - question: What is a minimum guarantee?
        answer: |
          An advance paid by a distributor or sales agent for the rights to a film. They pay a fixed amount up front, which the producer can use to finance the film, and then keep the film's income in their territory until they have earned that amount back plus their fees.
      - question: What is an indie film?
        answer: |
          An independent film, made outside the big studios and streamers. The production company raises the money from several sources (investors, film funds, broadcasters, distributors and tax incentives) and keeps more control over the film. "Indie" is about who owns and finances the film, not about its style. See [studio films and indie films](#kinds-of-production).
      - question: What is the difference between scripted and unscripted?
        answer: |
          Scripted productions are written in advance and performed by actors, like feature films and drama series. Unscripted productions follow real people without a script, like documentaries, reality and game shows. In unscripted the story is shaped in the edit, so post-production takes a bigger share of the time and the budget.
      - question: Is a TV series made the same way as a film?
        answer: |
          The crafts are the same, the money is different. A TV series is usually commissioned by a broadcaster or streamer that pays a licence fee for most of the cost. The production company then covers the rest with deficit financing. See [how a TV commission works](#tv-commission).
