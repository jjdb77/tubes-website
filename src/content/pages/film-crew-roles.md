  - type: textblock
    theme: white
    heading: How a production is organised
    lead: Titles differ per country, per union and per format, but the structure is roughly the same everywhere.
    text: |
      A production splits into **above the line** and **below the line**. Above the line are the people who shape the project creatively and are usually hired first, often on a fixed fee. Think of the producers, the director, the writer and the principal cast. Below the line is everyone else who makes the film physically happen, mostly paid by the day or the week. The split comes from the old budget topsheet, where a literal line separated the two. Our article on [above the line vs below the line](/insights/above-the-line-vs-below-the-line/) goes deeper.

      Below the line, the crew is grouped in **departments**. Each has a head (director of photography, production designer, costume designer and so on) who hires their own team and manages that part of the budget. The production office ties the departments together, and the production accountant keeps track of what they spend.

      A production also runs in **phases**. Development and pre-production are small teams, the shoot is when the crew is at its largest, and post-production shrinks back to an editor and a handful of specialists. Who is on the payroll changes week by week, which is why crew and schedule drive most of the budget.
  - type: textblock
    theme: light
    heading: Producers
    anchor: producers
    lead: The people who own the project, raise the money and answer for the result.
    plain_list: true
    two_column: true
    text: |
      - **Executive producer.** Often represents the money, such as a financier, a studio, a broadcaster or a company that brought in a large part of the funding. Approves the big decisions, rarely runs the day to day. On TV series the title is also used for the showrunner and senior writers.
      - **Producer.** Responsible for the project from idea to delivery. Secures rights, raises finance, hires the director and key creatives, and is accountable to financiers for budget and schedule. On a small production the producer does much of what the roles below do.
      - **Co-producer.** A partner who brings something in, typically money, a territory or facilities. In an international co-production the co-producer in each country handles the local part of the shoot and the incentive that comes with it.
      - **Line producer.** Turns the script into a budget and a schedule and then runs the production against them. Hires most of the crew below the line, negotiates with suppliers and signs off on costs. If something will go over, the line producer is usually the first to know and the one who has to find the money elsewhere.
      - **Unit production manager (UPM).** In the US system, the person who manages the physical production and its budget day to day, reporting to the line producer. In smaller productions the line producer and UPM are the same person.
      - **Associate producer.** A broad title. It can mean someone who did real producing work on a part of the project, or someone who helped bring in money or access. Read it as a credit, not a job description.
      - **Production manager.** The European counterpart of the UPM. Handles crew contracts, equipment, logistics and the cost of all of it, and manages the production office.
  - type: textblock
    theme: white
    heading: Direction and writing
    plain_list: true
    two_column: true
    text: |
      - **Writer / screenwriter.** Writes the screenplay, often through several drafts in development. On a series there is a writers' room led by a head writer or showrunner.
      - **Showrunner.** The lead of a TV series, combining head writer and executive producer. Decides on scripts, casting and edit across all episodes, while individual episodes can have different directors.
      - **Director.** Responsible for the creative vision on screen. Works with the cast on performance, with the director of photography on the image and with the editor on the cut. Has a say in casting and key crew.
      - **First assistant director (1st AD).** Runs the set and the shooting schedule. Breaks down the script, builds the schedule with the line producer and keeps the day on time. When a day runs over, the 1st AD is the one calling it.
      - **Second assistant director (2nd AD).** Writes the call sheet for each day, manages cast arrivals, make-up times and background actors, and is the link between set and production office.
      - **Script supervisor.** Tracks continuity, such as which hand an actor held the cup in and how much water was in the glass, and logs every take with notes for the editor.
      - **Casting director.** Finds and proposes actors for each role, organises auditions and negotiates deals with agents within the cast budget.
  - type: textblock
    theme: light
    heading: Production office and finance
    lead: The part of the crew that never appears on screen and keeps the rest of it working.
    plain_list: true
    two_column: true
    text: |
      - **Production coordinator.** Runs the production office. Books travel and accommodation, distributes scripts and schedules, handles crew paperwork and makes sure the right equipment shows up on the right day.
      - **Production assistant (PA) / runner.** The entry-level job on most productions. Office PAs support the coordinator, set PAs support the ADs. Many heads of department started here.
      - **Production accountant.** Keeps the books of the production. Processes purchase orders and invoices, manages petty cash, pays crew, reconciles bank accounts and reports actual costs against the budget every week in the [cost report](/insights/what-is-a-production-cost-report/). On larger productions there are assistant accountants and a separate payroll accountant.
      - **Payroll accountant.** Calculates and pays crew wages, overtime, holiday pay and the fringes that come on top (social charges, pension, union contributions). Rules differ per country, so this is a specialist role.
      - **Location manager.** Finds locations, negotiates fees and permits with owners and authorities, and manages the location on the day, from parking and power to the neighbours and leaving it as it was. Our [location guide](/compare-film-tv-locations/) has close to three thousand places to start from.
      - **Production lawyer / business affairs.** Drafts and checks contracts with cast, crew, rights holders and financiers. Often external, but a fixed line in the budget.
      - **Completion guarantor.** Not crew, but present on larger independent films. Guarantees financiers the film will be delivered, and receives the cost report every week to check that it will.
  - type: textblock
    theme: white
    heading: Camera, lighting and grip
    plain_list: true
    two_column: true
    text: |
      - **Director of photography (DP / DoP).** Head of the camera and lighting departments. Designs the look of the film with the director, chooses camera and lenses, and decides how each scene is lit.
      - **Camera operator.** Operates the camera during takes. On smaller productions the DP operates too.
      - **First assistant camera (1st AC / focus puller).** Keeps the image sharp during each take and is responsible for the camera kit.
      - **Second assistant camera (2nd AC / clapper loader).** Operates the slate, keeps the camera reports and manages media cards.
      - **DIT (digital imaging technician).** Backs up and checks the footage on set, applies a working colour look and prepares files for the edit.
      - **Gaffer.** Head of the electrical department. Turns the DP's lighting plan into lights, cables and power, including generators.
      - **Best boy.** Second in command to the gaffer (best boy electric) or the key grip (best boy grip). Handles crew, equipment orders and timesheets for the department.
      - **Key grip.** Head of the grip department, responsible for everything that holds or moves the camera and shapes the light, such as dollies, cranes, rigging and flags.
      - **Dolly grip.** Pushes the dolly during moving shots, hitting the same marks take after take.
  - type: textblock
    theme: light
    heading: Art, costume, hair and make-up
    plain_list: true
    two_column: true
    text: |
      - **Production designer.** Responsible for the look of everything in front of the camera apart from the actors, including sets, locations, props and colour. Manages the art department budget, often one of the largest below the line.
      - **Art director.** Turns the production designer's ideas into drawings, schedules and builds, and manages the art department crew.
      - **Set decorator.** Furnishes the sets with everything that is not built, like furniture, curtains, books and pictures.
      - **Props master.** Sources and manages every object an actor handles, and keeps duplicates for repeated takes.
      - **Construction manager.** Leads the carpenters, painters and plasterers who build the sets.
      - **Costume designer.** Designs, buys, rents or makes the costumes for every character, including multiples for stunts and continuity.
      - **Wardrobe supervisor.** Manages costumes during the shoot, including fittings, laundry, continuity and dressing the cast on set.
      - **Make-up and hair designer.** Designs the looks for each character, from natural make-up to wigs, ageing and prosthetics, and leads the make-up and hair team on set.
  - type: textblock
    theme: white
    heading: Sound, stunts and effects on set
    plain_list: true
    two_column: true
    text: |
      - **Production sound mixer.** Records dialogue and sound on set and decides which microphones go where.
      - **Boom operator.** Holds the boom microphone just out of frame.
      - **Stunt coordinator.** Designs and supervises every stunt, hires stunt performers and is responsible for their safety.
      - **Special effects (SFX) supervisor.** Creates practical effects on set, like rain, wind, smoke, fire, breakaway furniture and explosions. Not to be confused with VFX, which are added in post.
      - **Transport captain.** Organises the vehicles that move crew, cast and equipment between base and set, and the drivers.
      - **Health and safety advisor / set medic.** Assesses risks before the shoot and stays on set for dangerous days. In many countries this role is a legal requirement.
  - type: textblock
    theme: light
    heading: Post-production
    lead: The film is made a second time in the edit.
    plain_list: true
    two_column: true
    text: |
      - **Post-production supervisor.** Plans and runs post-production, with its schedule, facilities, deliveries and budget. Reports to the producer.
      - **Editor.** Assembles the footage into the film with the director, scene by scene and cut by cut. Often starts during the shoot with an assembly of each day's footage.
      - **Assistant editor.** Ingests and organises the footage, syncs sound, and prepares the files for every department that comes after the edit.
      - **VFX supervisor.** Plans visual effects with the director before the shoot, supervises shots on set and manages the VFX vendors in post. VFX budgets are usually per shot.
      - **Colourist.** Grades the image, setting the final colour and contrast of every shot in the grading suite.
      - **Sound designer and re-recording mixer.** The sound team builds the soundtrack from dialogue, effects, foley and music, and the re-recording mixer balances it into the final mix.
      - **Composer.** Writes the score. The music supervisor clears licensed songs, which has its own line in the budget.
  - type: textblock
    theme: white
    heading: Other formats use other titles
    text: |
      **TV series** add the showrunner and writers' room, and often a producer per block of episodes. The line producer and accountant work across all episodes, which makes cost reporting per episode and per block a real job.

      **Commercials** have two sides. The agency producer works for the advertising agency and the brand. The production company has its own executive producer, producer and production manager, and quotes a fixed price based on a bid. The margin on that bid is often what the production company lives on.

      **Documentaries** have small crews where one person does several jobs. The director may also produce and do sound, and a shooting producer films and interviews at the same time. Budgets are lower, but the schedule is less predictable.

      **Unscripted and entertainment** shows have a series producer, story producers and edit producers who build the episode from many hours of footage.
  - type: textblock
    theme: light
    heading: Who signs off on the money
    anchor: money
    text: |
      Every role above spends money. A production stays on budget when it is clear who may spend what, and who sees it afterwards.

      - **Heads of department** manage their own budget line. They request purchase orders, collect quotes and approve their team's timesheets.
      - **The line producer or production manager** approves purchase orders above a set amount and decides where money moves when one department needs more.
      - **The production accountant** records every commitment and invoice, and once a week reports the actual cost plus what is still to come against the budget. That gives the estimate for completion (EFC).
      - **The producer** answers to financiers and the completion guarantor, and approves the use of [contingency](/insights/how-much-contingency-does-a-production-need/).

      On many productions this runs through email, spreadsheets and a shared folder, and the cost report is days behind the set. Tubes keeps budget, schedule, purchase orders and actual costs in one place, with approvals per role, so every person in this chain works from the same numbers.
  - type: faq
    theme: white
    heading: Questions about crew roles
    items:
      - question: What is the difference between a producer and a line producer?
        answer: |
          The producer owns the project, from rights and financing to key creatives and delivery to financiers. The line producer runs the production physically, building the budget and schedule from the script and managing the crew and costs against them. On small productions one person does both.
      - question: What is the difference between a line producer and a production manager?
        answer: |
          They overlap. The line producer is responsible for budget and schedule overall, and the production manager runs the daily logistics and the office, reporting to the line producer. In the US system the production manager is called the unit production manager (UPM).
      - question: What does a production accountant do?
        answer: |
          Keeps the books of one production. That covers purchase orders, invoices, crew payments, petty cash, bank reconciliation and a weekly cost report that compares actual and committed costs with the budget. On a larger production there is a team with assistants and a payroll accountant.
      - question: What do the gaffer and best boy do?
        answer: |
          The gaffer is the head of the electrical department and turns the DP's lighting plan into lights, cables and power. The best boy is the gaffer's second in command, handling the crew, equipment orders and paperwork. The grip department has its own best boy who works for the key grip.
      - question: What is the difference between SFX and VFX?
        answer: |
          SFX (special effects) happen physically on set, like rain, smoke, fire and explosions. VFX (visual effects) are created digitally in post-production and added to the footage. A scene can use both.
      - question: Which roles are above the line?
        answer: |
          Producers, the director, the writer and the principal cast, plus their travel and assistants. Everyone else is below the line. See [above the line vs below the line in a film budget](/insights/above-the-line-vs-below-the-line/) for how that split works in the budget.
      - question: How many crew does a film need?
        answer: |
          It depends on the format. A small documentary can work with three or four people, an independent feature typically has 30 to 80 crew on the shoot, and a large studio film can have several hundred across all units. The schedule and the number of shooting days matter as much as the headcount, because most crew are paid per day or per week.
