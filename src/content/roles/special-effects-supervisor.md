It is a dry summer evening, but the script wants a storm. The special effects supervisor has rain towers on cranes above the street, a wind machine at the corner, and a team ready to make the shop sign swing and fall on cue. When the director calls action, the storm starts. When the director calls cut, it stops. The special effects (SFX) supervisor is the person responsible for effects that physically happen on set, as opposed to visual effects added later on a computer.

## What a special effects supervisor does

The SFX supervisor works for the [director](/production-roles/director/) and the [producer](/production-roles/producer/) and joins in [pre-production](/how-a-film-is-made/#pre-production). They read the script for anything that has to burn, explode, break, blow, pour or move by itself, and propose how to do it. Some effects are built in a workshop, others are rigged on location or on set. A breakaway table, a car that flips, snow on a summer street and a cup that rattles on a shaking table are all SFX work.

A large part of the job is deciding with the [VFX supervisor](/production-roles/vfx-supervisor/) what is done for real and what is finished digitally. A real explosion with digital debris added later is a common mix. On the day, the SFX team rigs, tests and triggers each effect, often together with the [stunt coordinator](/production-roles/stunt-coordinator/) when people are close to it.

## Responsibilities

- Break down the script for physical effects
- Design and build rigs, mechanical effects and breakaway objects
- Create weather on set, including rain, snow, wind, fog and smoke
- Plan and carry out pyrotechnics and fire effects where they are allowed
- Agree with the VFX supervisor where physical effects end and digital effects begin
- Write risk assessments with the [health and safety advisor](/production-roles/health-and-safety-advisor/) and handle any permits
- Run the SFX crew and equipment on shoot days

## How the role touches the budget

The SFX supervisor prices each effect and manages the SFX budget for crew, equipment, materials and consumables. Some effects are cheap to rig but slow to reset, such as rain that soaks the set and costumes. Others are expensive to build and can only be done once, like a car that goes through a wall. The choice between practical and digital effects is often a budget conversation with the [line producer](/production-roles/line-producer/), because one moves cost into production and the other into post-production.

## How people become SFX supervisors

Most start as SFX trainees or technicians, often with a background in engineering, mechanics, welding, carpentry or electrics. Working with explosives and pyrotechnics usually requires specific licences or certification, which differ between countries. Supervisors typically have many years in an SFX crew before they lead one.

## In TV and commercials

TV series often reuse effects across episodes, such as a regular rain set-up. In commercials SFX work is often very precise and product focused, for example liquid splashes or a perfectly timed puff of steam, and the effect may be repeated many times until it looks exactly right.
