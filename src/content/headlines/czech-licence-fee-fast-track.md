The Czech governing coalition has pressed ahead with consideration of a proposal to abolish compulsory contributions for Česká televize and Český rozhlas. The public broadcasters would henceforth be funded directly from the state budget, raising concerns about their independence.

The government wants to replace the current television and radio contributions with funding from the state budget. According to the parliamentary report, the Czech Lower House gave the proposal priority at its first reading on Friday; the opposition failed to reverse that procedure. The law has therefore not yet been passed, but an important parliamentary step has been taken.

According to the European Broadcasting Union (EBU), the combined budget of the two public media organisations would be around 1.4 billion Czech korunas lower. That is approximately 15 per cent less than the amount on which the broadcasters currently operate. Parliamentary documents refer to an annual state contribution of about 7.8 billion korunas.

The coalition defends the new model as a way to make funding simpler and more modern. SPD MP Josef Nerušil told Czech public radio that the current system is outdated and that the coalition is fulfilling an election promise. The government also argues that the state ought to pay for the public service.

Opposition parties instead see a risk that the broadcasters will become financially dependent on the government and the parliament of the day. According to STAN MPs, the speed with which the proposal is being considered threatens open debate. The discussion is therefore not only about the size of the contribution, but also about who determines the conditions for public-service journalism.

Employees of Česká televize and Český rozhlas are also opposed. The initiative group Veřejnoprávně has announced a nationwide strike for 3 November. According to the group, nearly 3,200 of approximately 4,250 employees have backed the call; the precise consequences for broadcasts will be announced later.

The EBU and international organisations for journalism and press freedom warn that direct budget funding could weaken the independence and stability of the two broadcasters. They also point to the short time available for consideration. The criticism is not proof that the broadcasters are losing their independence, but it does indicate that the reform is being closely monitored at European level.

The development is relevant to the Netherlands because Czechia is part of the European debate on public media, media freedom and stable funding. The proposal still has to pass through the remainder of the legislative process. It is not yet clear whether parliament will ultimately approve it or what safeguards to keep the broadcasters at arm’s length from the government will be included.
